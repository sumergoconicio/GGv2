# *11th March 2017, Day 7*

![2017-06-12 06.53.26 1535309753064154970_5025663047.jpg](/archive/media/d357577cb089895bbd7f.jpg)

The next morning, we awoke to an announcement by the expedition leader that we were entering one of the most beautiful channels in the Antarctic Peninsula. If you recall, a channel is a body of water surrounded on two sides by land. In this case, there were towering cliffs on both sides, peaks in the clouds, as we drifted past in silent awe. Ok, maybe we weren't that silent...

Which brings me to a recurrent thought I've always entertained: that the universe is always magical, but not always in the timescales humans can perceive.

Mountains are alive, they are born, they grow, they wrinkle and then they pass on - but in the timespan of billions of years. Entire generations of quantum reactions happen within the blink of an eye. The sun is a slow-motion nuclear explosion and we're merely sunbathing in its eon-spanning plume.

Humanity itself hasn't stood still over the centuries: we were like babes once grasping at stones, experienced teenage existential angst during the Dark Ages and are now entering a formative time when we choose the life purpose of our species. Every spoken word is a magic spell that enters others' ears, hearts and minds, influencing decisions and outcomes that sometimes change the very course of history.

The universe *is* magical, but only for those who haven't forgotten the joy of awe and wonder.

Standing in line before the dip, my mind was dizzy with imagining what it would be like. I was somewhat comforted by the dozen or so people who jumped before me - and I figured, if they survived I should be fine too. Small comfort.

How best to describe the feeling? It was cold. A shock to the nervous system. I unwittingly swallowed some Antarctic water - more salty than I remember seawater being. The cold reached my bones. But the 20 seconds I was in water, I was almost getting comfortably numb. Then the expedition personnel helped to pull me out with the red cord.

As I started up the ladder, it wasn't the cold but the heat of the ambient air that was excruciating. My nerves were on fire. For about the next 30 seconds, I couldn't really move. Every inch of skin was registering a signal and my brain felt overloaded. Eventually, and rather quickly, the sensation passed of course, but while it lasted, it felt like a reboot of the system.

Since meaning-making is important to me, I refer to this as my baptism by ice to the Church of Earth.
